Research with the University of Chicago

A wearable measure of frailty for older adults receiving chemotherapy

Frailty biomarkerMeasured

Problem
One in four older adults receiving chemotherapy has an unplanned hospital admission (25.7% of 750 adults aged 65 and over; Klepin et al., 2021). Frailty helps predict who is at risk, but it is usually assessed once in clinic, if at all.
Approach
A watch records steps, sleep and overnight heart rate every day. If a decline from a person’s own baseline appears before an admission, a nurse could call that patient before it happens.
Aims to improve
Quality and cost: fewer avoidable admissions, without added clinic visits.
Status
  • MeasuredDaily step counts tracked the All of Us frailty index: about 8,100 steps a day in the fit group, 7,100 in the pre-frail group and 5,500 in the frail group. Published as an ASCO 2025 abstract.
  • PlannedThe next study is designed. It asks whether a decline from a person’s own baseline precedes a hospital admission, and the result is publishable whether or not it does.
  • Not yet measuredWhether the signal predicts an admission.
Next steps
Agree on the scope and roles for the next study with Dr. Mir, then run it in All of Us. A pilot is proposed for 2027.
Looking for
Introductions to people who run community or geriatric oncology programs.
Team
Dr. Nabiel Mir, Geriatric Oncology, University of Chicago, is the lead investigator. Kenan Najjar runs the analysis. I work on the clinical framing and translation.

Clinical tool, developed during my psychiatry clerkship

A between-visit symptom diary for outpatient psychiatry

IntervalBuilt

Problem
Measurement-based care improves outcomes. In one randomized trial, 73.8% of patients with major depression reached remission, compared with 28.8% under standard care (Guo et al., 2015). Yet fewer than one in five behavioral health clinicians use it (Lewis et al., 2019). Visits are short, and patients are asked to recall from memory how they felt in the weeks since the last one.
Approach
Patients complete a one-minute daily check-in and weekly rating scales. Before each visit the clinician receives a summary, ordered the way a psychiatrist would ask the questions, so the visit starts with better information and takes no longer.
Aims to improve
Quality, without adding clinician time.
Status
  • BuiltA working demo: the patient check-in, 25 transparent rules anchored to the last visit, and a clinician panel whose summary can be pasted into the note. It uses no machine learning and makes no predictions. It has 108 automated tests.
  • BuiltThe patient chooses what the psychiatrist sees.
  • SimulatedIt has been run only on four synthetic patients.
  • Not yet measuredUse with real patients. None has used it.
Next steps
A supervising psychiatrist reviews the rules and the mania scale, and the hospital determines whether a pilot counts as quality improvement or research. A pilot with 10 to 20 patients would follow.
Looking for
A supervising psychiatrist willing to review the rules.
Team
I built it during my psychiatry clerkship.

A digital twin for heart disease prevention, reviewed by the patient’s own cardiologist

Digital twinBuilt

Problem
Nearly half of US adults have high blood pressure, and about one in four of them have it under control (CDC). Published equations can turn a person’s blood pressure, cholesterol and other routine measurements into an estimate of their risk over ten and thirty years (Khan et al., 2024). That estimate helps only if the person sees it and a clinician who knows them decides what to do about it.
Approach
A person answers a short survey and adds whatever measurements they have. The twin estimates their risk of heart attack, stroke and heart failure with the American Heart Association’s PREVENT equations, projects it year by year, and shows what would change the projection. Coded guideline rules prepare a one-page brief for the person’s own cardiologist, who decides what goes into the plan.
Aims to improve
Quality and access: guideline-based prevention that begins at home and is decided by the patient’s own cardiologist.
Status
  • BuiltA prototype that runs in a web browser on five synthetic people. Its estimates are calculated from equations and coded rules, with no language model involved, so the same inputs always give the same result.
  • BuiltThe PREVENT calculation matches the worked example published with preventr, an R package that implements the equations.
  • SimulatedImporting lab results, syncing devices, the optional DNA test, sending the brief and the cardiologist’s release are all simulated. Nothing is sent and no health record system is connected.
  • Not yet measuredUse by real patients. It has not been tested with people, and its clinical rules and thresholds are placeholders that no physician has signed off. It is not a medical device.
  • Not yet measuredAccuracy of the year-by-year projection. It follows the LIFE-CVD2 method (Hageman et al., 2024), which was developed for European populations, and has not been checked in a US cohort.
Next steps
A physician lead reviews and signs off every rule and threshold, and the projection is calibrated in a US cohort. A regulatory review and a written pilot with one cardiology clinic would come before any real patient. A companion study in the NIH All of Us Research Program asks how often the inputs to a risk estimate are on file in routine care, how long it takes to go from a cholesterol result to treatment, and what wearable data add. That study is research in its own right, and its results will not be used to promote the product.
Team
I work on it with co-founders and a development team. As of November 2026, no company has been formed and the project has no outside funding.

Family screening for inherited heart risk, starting from a college student’s result

KinlinePlanned

Problem
About 1 in 250 people has familial hypercholesterolemia, an inherited cause of early heart disease, and most do not know it. When one person is diagnosed, relatives should be tested. Even an organized outreach program using several channels led to testing in only about a fifth of first-degree relatives (IMPACT-FH, 2024).
Approach
A college student can reach two parents. If the student receives the result and chooses to invite them, the family can be tested without a new clinic.
Aims to improve
Access, without a new clinic.
Status
  • PlannedThe program design is written and has been checked against its own rules. Relatives receive no risk scores or treatment advice, and sponsors receive no identifiable data.
  • PlannedA companion research letter in All of Us asks whether inherited lipid risk is recognized and treated in adults under 50 who already have cardiovascular disease. A first draft was written in November 2026. Its results stay off this site until the letter is published.
  • SimulatedConcept screens with a fictional family.
  • Not yet measuredEverything else. A faculty PI, IRB approval and a first participant are all still ahead, and Kinline is not enrolling anyone.
Next steps
A first pre-test with parents is targeted for December 2026. On January 29, 2027, the project continues only if five conditions are met: a faculty PI and an IRB path, a written statement from a partner, three of ten parents completing the pre-test, the companion letter in co-author review, and a time commitment under about six hours a week for each of us.
Looking for
A faculty PI, confirmed in writing, with an IRB path.
Team
I lead the clinical side. Kenan Najjar leads the data analysis.

Next steps
Finalize the exposure and outcome definitions. A first abstract is targeted for early 2027.

Software product

A scheduling and record-keeping app for the clinical years of medical school

ChartedBuilt

Problem
In the clinical years, schedules arrive as spreadsheets, the rules are spread across 70 separate policies, and the experiences a residency application will ask about are easy to forget by the next rotation. At my school, clerkship schedules are assigned by lottery into fixed tracks.
Approach
Student preference and clerkship staffing are usually treated as a trade-off. A matching algorithm can serve both, giving every student a custom schedule while every clerkship stays staffed.
Status
  • BuiltA working app: the day’s schedule with location and preceptor, every deadline a rotation triggers, 70 school policies searchable with cited answers, and a dictated debrief journal that removes patient identifiers and builds a file for residency applications.
  • MeasuredOne user so far. I have used it daily since October 2026.
  • SimulatedKenan Najjar’s clerkship match, in 90 simulated classes of 64 students: 73% received everything feasible for them, compared with 38% under fixed tracks.
  • Not yet measuredReal student preferences, and any user other than me.
Next steps
Testing with classmates and interviews through December 2026. A pilot alongside a real lottery cycle is proposed for early 2027 and depends on the school’s agreement.
Looking for
Classmates willing to test it, and a school willing to run the match alongside its lottery.
Team
I build the product. Kenan Najjar built the match.

Approach

William Kissick described the iron triangle in 1994 in Medicine’s Dilemmas. A health system pursues three goals: access, quality and cost containment. His argument was that they compete. Covering more people raises cost, and reducing cost tends to limit access or quality.

I think that trade-off is strongest when the only tool is money, and weaker when the tool is information. Several of my projects start from the same observation: something useful is already known, and it does not reach the person who could act on it. A student’s cholesterol result is relevant to her parents. A watch already records the step counts that track frailty. Published equations can estimate a person’s risk of heart disease from routine measurements, but the estimate is of little use unless that person and their doctor see it. Getting that information to the right person costs little, which is why I think these are places where one corner of the triangle can improve without the others getting worse.

I came to each of these problems through experience: a clerkship, a research group or a company I started. I think of each project as a lever on one corner of the triangle, and of that experience as the ground the lever stands on. The diagram on the home page shows both.

Most of these projects are at an early stage. Each is labelled with one of four terms so that its stage is clear.

Planned
Designed and documented. Nothing has been built and there are no data.
Simulated
Tested on synthetic or invented inputs.
Built
Working software that a person can use today.
Measured
Results from real data or real use. Shown in pink.
